AI influencers are digital characters, generated or rendered with AI and CGI, that run social media accounts, post content and promote products the way a human creator does, except that a brand or studio controls every image, caption and voice line. Some are independent "stars" that brands pay for sponsored posts (Lil Miquela, Aitana López, Imma). Others are owned by a single company and act as a permanent brand face (Magazine Luiza's Lu do Magalu in Brazil). For a marketer, a virtual influencer trades the reach and lived credibility of a human creator for total control, no scheduling limits and no off-brand scandals. The trade comes with obligations: the FTC treats virtual endorsers like human ones, the EU AI Act now requires AI content disclosure, and Meta, TikTok and YouTube label synthetic media. To use AI influencers well, you need:

1. A clear role: rent an existing virtual influencer, or build an owned character.
2. A character bible that locks face, body, wardrobe, voice and personality.
3. A production pipeline that keeps the character consistent across hundreds of images and videos.
4. A disclosure plan that covers both the sponsorship and the fact that the character is not human.

## What are AI influencers?

"AI influencer" and "virtual influencer" are used interchangeably, but the technology has shifted. Early virtual influencers were built in 3D software and composited into photos. Most new characters are made with image and video models (Midjourney, Flux, Kling, Veo, Runway), with CGI and retouching to keep the identity stable.

### Virtual influencers vs. AI avatars

A virtual influencer has a persona: a name, a backstory, opinions and an audience that follows the character itself. An AI avatar is usually a presenter for a single video. The distinction matters for budget: a persona needs ongoing content and community management, an avatar does not.

### Known AI influencer examples

According to Built In's 2025 overview of the category, the best known virtual influencers include:

- **Lu do Magalu**, created by Brazilian retailer Magazine Luiza in 2003, with more than 8 million Instagram followers and campaigns with Adidas and Samsung. She is the clearest case of an owned brand character.
- **Lil Miquela**, created by the LA company Brud in 2016, with more than 2 million Instagram followers and work for Calvin Klein and Chanel.
- **Imma**, created by Tokyo CG studio ModelingCafe in 2018, with work for IKEA, Dior and Nike.
- **Aitana López**, created in 2023 by the Barcelona agency The Clueless, with campaigns for Guess and Brandy Melville.
- **Noonoouri**, created by Joerg Zuber in 2018, who signed a record deal with Warner Music in 2023.

Aitana López shows the business model behind the newer, fully AI characters. According to Euronews, her creators say she earns around €3,000 a month on average and up to €10,000, with just over €1,000 per advertisement, and that the agency built her after too many projects were delayed or cancelled because of human talent.

## How brands use AI influencers

There are three common setups, and each one has a different cost profile and risk.

### Sponsored posts with an existing virtual influencer

You brief the character's owner as you would a creator's manager. You get an existing audience, but you do not own the character or control its reputation.

### An owned brand character

You build a character that lives on your channels, or its own account, for years. Lu do Magalu is the model: a mascot with a social presence. It is the highest upfront investment and the strongest long-term asset.

### Campaign talent without a persona

Many brands do not want a public "influencer" at all. They want a consistent AI model to wear the collection in a lookbook, star in product videos or appear in paid social, without the posting and community side. This overlaps with [AI fashion models](/blog/ai-fashion-models) and with [AI UGC ads](/blog/ai-ugc-ads), where the character speaks to camera like a customer. Because the character is synthetic, you can also version it per market without reshooting.

## Virtual influencer vs. human influencer

| Criteria | Virtual (AI) influencer | Human influencer |
|---|---|---|
| Control over content | Total: every frame and caption is approved | Shared: the creator keeps their voice |
| Availability | Unlimited, any location, any season | Limited by schedule, travel, health |
| Reputation risk | No personal scandals, but backlash against AI itself | Personal behavior can hit the brand |
| Audience trust | Must be earned; product experience claims are restricted | Lived experience is the main asset |
| Ownership | You can own the character and its IP (by contract) | You rent access to a person |
| Disclosure | Sponsorship plus "AI-generated" or "virtual" status | Sponsorship disclosure |
| Content cost over time | High setup, lower marginal cost per post | Fee per deliverable, rises with reach |
| Consistency | Requires a strict pipeline to avoid face drift | Natural |

A virtual influencer is strongest for fashion, beauty, tech and entertainment brands that sell an image or a world, and weakest where the endorsement depends on real-body results, such as skincare, supplements or fitness.

## How to create an AI influencer

Building a character is a design project first. Most AI influencers fail on consistency and personality, not image quality.

### 1. Write the character bible

The character bible is the reference document every image, video and caption is checked against. It should cover:

- **Identity:** name, age range, city, backstory, values, what the character would never do.
- **Face and body:** a locked set of reference images (front, three-quarter, profile), skin details, hair, height and proportions.
- **Personality and voice:** tone of captions, vocabulary, humor, the topics it posts about.
- **Brand fit:** which products it can credibly feature, and which claims it must never make.

### 2. Lock the face for consistency

Face drift is the most visible failure. Teams combine a fixed reference set with the character reference features of their tools, or train a custom model on approved images, and check every output against the sheet. Expect to reject many generations; hands, teeth and jewelry still need retouching.

### 3. Build the wardrobe and world

Define a wardrobe (signature pieces, palette, brands it wears) and recurring locations, so the feed feels like one life. A featured product is safer modeled in 3D or photographed and composited, because generation models still distort logos and labels.

### 4. Give it a voice

If the character speaks, lock one voice, generated with a tool such as ElevenLabs or recorded by a voice actor under contract. Never clone a real person's voice without written consent.

### 5. Set a posting system

Plan a cadence you can sustain, a content mix (editorials, short videos, sponsored posts), and someone who replies to comments in the character's voice. Community management is a recurring cost that brands often underestimate.

## Disclosure rules for AI influencers

### FTC Endorsement Guides (US)

The FTC's 2023 update to the Endorsement Guides expanded the definition of an endorser to include anything that appears to be an individual, which covers virtual influencers. As BakerLaw's analysis summarizes, the brand relationship must be clearly disclosed, the character should state it is virtual when that is not obvious, and it must not imply personal experience it cannot have (for example, that a moisturizer cleared its skin). Brands and agencies remain responsible for monitoring posts.

### EU AI Act transparency rules

Article 50 of the EU AI Act has applied since August 2, 2026. According to the practical guide published by artificialintelligenceact.eu, anyone who publishes AI-generated or manipulated images, audio or video that would falsely appear authentic must disclose it clearly, at the latest at first exposure. A photorealistic AI human presented to EU audiences falls in this scope. Evidently artistic or fictional work gets a lighter duty, but disclosure is still required in a way that does not spoil the work.

### Platform labels

Meta renamed its "Made with AI" label to "AI info" in 2024 after complaints about mislabeling, according to PetaPixel, and it relies on industry metadata to detect AI content. TikTok requires creators to label realistic AI-generated content and reads C2PA Content Credentials to flag content made elsewhere, as SiliconANGLE reported. YouTube requires disclosure of realistic altered or synthetic content in YouTube Studio, per its Help Center. In practice: state "AI-generated" or "virtual creator" in the bio, use the platform's AI label, and keep standard ad disclosure for sponsored posts.

## Costs and risks of AI influencers

Costs fall into three blocks: character development (one-time), content production (scales with output) and operation (posting, community, reporting, monthly).

The main risks to plan for:

- **Audience backlash.** Some audiences reject AI characters on principle. Test with paid social before committing to a long-term persona.
- **Uncanny or inconsistent output.** One off-model face in a feed breaks the illusion. Quality control is the job.
- **Rights.** Make sure your contract assigns the character's IP, reference images and voice to you, and that no real person's likeness was used to build it. The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that prompts alone do not make you the author of an output (as Jones Day summarizes), so human design and editing work strengthens your position.
- **Misleading claims.** Keep the character away from claims that require a human body or personal use.

## FAQ

### What is an AI influencer?

An AI influencer is a digital character created with AI image, video and voice tools, sometimes combined with CGI, that publishes social content and promotes products like a human creator. A company or studio controls its look, posts and partnerships. Well-known examples include Lil Miquela, Imma and Aitana López.

### Do AI influencers have to disclose that they are AI?

In the US, the FTC's Endorsement Guides say a virtual endorser should make its nature clear when it is not obvious, and sponsorships must always be disclosed. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act requires disclosure of realistic AI-generated content. Meta, TikTok and YouTube also expect or apply AI labels.

### How much does an AI influencer make?

Few creators publish figures. According to Euronews, the agency behind Aitana López says she averages around €3,000 a month, with peaks up to €10,000. Characters with millions of followers command more.

### Can I create an AI influencer for my brand?

Yes. You need a character bible, locked face references, a wardrobe and voice, a pipeline that keeps the character consistent, and a posting plan. Most brands hire a studio for design and production and keep approvals in-house.

### Are AI influencers better than human influencers?

They are better at control, availability and consistency, and worse at lived credibility. For products whose value depends on real-world results, human creators remain more convincing and easier to keep compliant.
