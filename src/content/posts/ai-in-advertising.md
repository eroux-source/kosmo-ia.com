AI in advertising now mostly means generative AI used to make the ads themselves: concept frames, footage, voices, music and the dozens of versions a campaign needs for each platform and audience. Media buying and targeting have used machine learning for a decade; what changed since 2024 is that the creative work moved into the same loop. According to IAB's 2025 video ad spend report, half of US video ad buyers already use generative AI to build video ads, and buyers expect AI-built creative to reach 40% of all video ads by 2026. In practice, generative AI changes four things:

1. **Concept:** you can see a film as moving frames before anyone commits a production budget.
2. **Production:** scenes that would need a location, a crew or a set can be generated, then finished by artists.
3. **Versioning:** one master becomes many cuts, formats, languages and hooks at a lower cost per version.
4. **Personalization:** versions can be matched to audiences, contexts and offers instead of one ad for everyone.

What it does not change: you still need an idea, an accurate product, clear usage rights and a team that can fix what the models get wrong.

## How generative AI advertising changes the creative process

The biggest shift is where time and money go. A traditional shoot front-loads cost into one production window; generative AI advertising spreads the work across iterations, so you can afford to explore before you commit and to adapt after launch.

### Concept and pre-production

Image models such as Midjourney and Flux let a creative team turn a script into a set of styled frames in days, not weeks. Video models such as Veo, Kling, Runway and Sora can then animate the strongest frames into short motion tests. The result is a "moving mood board" a client can approve with far less guesswork than a static deck.

AI is most reliable here: a concept frame only needs to convey light, cast, setting and pace.

### Production

In production, generation replaces some or all of the camera work. A fashion film can be set on a dry lake, a product can float over a landscape, a travel ad can open on any beach. Voices can come from tools like ElevenLabs, and music from tools like Suno, subject to their licensing terms.

The limits are real, though. Current models still drift on faces, hands, logos, typography and product details from one shot to the next. For brand work, the product usually has to be modeled or composited from real references, and every shot needs grading, cleanup and an edit. Treat raw generation as footage, not as a finished ad.

### Versioning and localization

This is where the economics change most. The IAB report found that advertisers using generative AI for video apply it to create audience-specific versions (42%), change visual styles (38%) and improve contextual relevance (36%). Once a world and a cast exist, a new aspect ratio, a seasonal variant or a language version is mostly an edit, not a reshoot.

Platforms are pushing the same direction. eMarketer reported in 2026 that Meta expanded its Advantage+ generative tools at the IAB NewFronts, including AI voiceovers and one-step translation of creative in Ads Manager, and that advertisers in a beta who used AI video generation for most of their campaign ads saw average gains of 10% in click-through rate and 8% in conversion rate, according to Meta.

### Personalization

Personalization at the creative level means you can match the setting, the offer or the opening line to a segment. The catch is that versions multiply your review work: every variant needs a brand, legal and accuracy check. The WARC and TikTok study published in July 2026 found that 88% of marketers report higher creative volume from generative AI, but only 45% report a significant quality improvement. More versions only help if each one is still good.

## Risks: rights, disclosure and brand safety

Generative AI moves risk from the set to the paperwork. Most of the problems below are avoidable if they are handled before production starts.

### Ownership and copyright

The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 (Part 2 of its AI report) that prompts alone do not make someone the author of an output. Protection applies where a human contributes enough expressive elements, for example through selection, arrangement or modification of the material. For an ad, that means the human editing, compositing and design work matters, and your contract should state who owns the final assets and what tools and licenses were used.

### Likeness and synthetic performers

Never generate a recognizable real person without written consent. New York went further: a law signed in December 2025 and in force since June 9, 2026 requires advertisers to conspicuously disclose synthetic performers (digitally created humans that are not an identifiable real person) when they know an ad uses one, with civil penalties of $1,000 for a first violation and $5,000 for each one after, according to Cooley's analysis. Ads for expressive works such as films and games are exempt when the performer is used the same way as in the work itself, and audio-only ads are not covered.

### Disclosure and platform labels

Platforms now label AI content in ads. Marketing Dive reported in July 2026 that Meta shows an AI disclosure in the "About this ad" menu when advertisers use its own generative tools, and when it detects third-party AI edits through standards such as C2PA. TikTok and YouTube have their own rules, covered in our guide to [AI video ads that perform on Meta, TikTok and YouTube](/blog/ai-video-ads).

### Fake testimonials

The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials, in effect since October 13, 2024, prohibits testimonials attributed to people who do not exist and reviews from people with no real experience of the product, and it allows civil penalties. An AI presenter can demonstrate a product; it cannot claim to be a satisfied customer.

### Brand safety and accuracy

Models can invent product features, distort packaging or produce scenes that look like someone else's campaign. Build a review step for product accuracy, claims, likeness and visual similarity to existing work into every round, not just the final one.

## How to choose an AI creative agency

An AI advertising agency can mean anything from a prompt operator to a full studio. Ask these questions before you sign:

### Can they show finished work, not just generations?

Look for edited, graded, sound-designed films with accurate products, and ask which parts were generated and which were built in 3D or composited.

### Who fixes what AI gets wrong?

Consistency of faces, products and logos across 20 shots is the hard part. An AI creative agency should have compositing, 3D and color people in house or in a named team, not only generalists.

### How do they handle rights and disclosure?

Ask which tools and licenses they use, whether they train or fine-tune on your assets, who owns the outputs, how they document consent for any likeness, and how they label content for each platform and market.

### Do they deliver for media, not just for the showreel?

You need every ratio (16:9, 9:16, 1:1, 4:5), cutdowns, hook variants and platform-safe text placement. Ask how versions are produced and how many review rounds are included.

### Is the price fixed and the scope clear?

Generation is cheap; iteration is not. A clear scope with a fixed price protects you from paying per attempt. For budget ranges, see our breakdown of [what an AI commercial costs](/blog/ai-commercial-cost).

## FAQ

### What is AI in advertising?

AI in advertising is the use of machine learning and generative AI across the ad process: targeting, bidding and measurement on the media side, and concepts, footage, voices and versions on the creative side. Since 2024 the creative use has grown fastest. IAB's 2025 research found that half of US video ad buyers already use generative AI to build video ads.

### Is generative AI advertising cheaper than a traditional shoot?

It often costs less per version and can avoid travel, sets and locations, but finishing still takes skilled people and time. The savings are largest when you need many versions, settings that would be expensive to film, or fast iteration. A single hero film with a real celebrity is usually still best shot traditionally.

### Do AI-generated ads need to be labeled?

It depends on the platform and the market. Meta, TikTok and YouTube each have disclosure rules for realistic AI content, and New York requires conspicuous disclosure of synthetic performers in ads since June 2026. Check every platform and state where the ad will run.

### Who owns an ad made with AI?

Under US Copyright Office guidance, prompts alone do not create authorship, but human selection, arrangement and modification can be protected. Ownership of the final assets should be set in your contract, along with the tools and licenses used. Ask your agency to document this before production.

### What should an AI advertising agency deliver?

Finished, edited films with accurate products, every format your media plan needs, cutdowns and test variants, plus documentation of tools, licenses and consents. If an agency only delivers raw generations, your team will carry the finishing and the legal risk.
